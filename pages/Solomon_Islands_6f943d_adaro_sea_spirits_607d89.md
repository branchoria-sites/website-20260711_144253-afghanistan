## Introduction

Many of the [Solomon Islands]({{ 'which-solomon-islands-creatures-are-myth/' | relative_url }})’ most famous “monster” stories did not begin as reports of unknown animals. They began as spiritual traditions that explained how people related to the sea, their ancestors and unseen forces. The adaro is one of the best-known examples. Often described in modern retellings as a frightening fish-human creature, the adaro originally belonged to a much broader system of belief on Makira Island, where spirits, ghosts and ancestral powers were woven into everyday life. Rather than being evidence for a hidden marine species, adaro traditions reveal how island communities understood danger, weather, fishing, death and the unpredictable nature of the ocean.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaro_%28mythology%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Adaro (mythology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">January 22, 2026 — Adaro is a term for two distinct classes of beings found in the mythology of Makira island, in the Solomon Islands. ## Who or What Is the Adaro?

The adaro is most strongly associated with Makira, formerly known as San Cristóbal, in the eastern Solomon Islands. Early ethnographic records describe the term as referring to powerful spiritual beings rather than ordinary creatures. In some traditions, adaro are linked to ghosts and the dangerous or destructive aspects of a person's spiritual nature. Other traditions describe elemental beings associated with the sea, weather and creation stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaro_%28mythology%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Adaro (mythology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">January 22, 2026 — Adaro is a term for two distinct classes of beings found in the mythology of Makira island, in the Solomon Islands. Descriptions recorded in folklore are striking. Adaro are often portrayed as human-like but with aquatic features such as gills, fin-like feet and a horn resembling the dorsal fin of a shark or the bill of a swordfish. They were said to travel through waterspouts, storms and even along rainbows. Stories portray them as dangerous beings capable of attacking fishermen, sometimes by launching poisonous flying fish as supernatural weapons.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nightbringer.se/nightbringer/lair_adaro.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nightbringer.se">[nightbringer.se]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nightbringer.se</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AdaroSea spirits in the mythology of the Solomon Islands. ## Why Sea Spirits Became Linked to the Ocean's Power

For island societies surrounded by water, the sea was both a source of survival and a place of constant risk. Fishing voyages could bring food and prosperity, but storms, currents and marine predators could also bring death. Spiritual traditions helped explain those uncertainties.

Historical accounts from the Solomon Islands and neighbouring parts of Melanesia describe widespread beliefs in spirits that could inhabit animals, especially sharks and other marine creatures. Some communities regarded certain sharks as manifestations of ancestral beings or ghosts. Others maintained traditions of sea spirits connected with important fishing activities, including bonito fishing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://anglicanhistory.org/oceania/artless1936/02.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: anglicanhistory.org">[Anglican History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">anglicanhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Anglican History The Church in Melanesia, edited by Stuart W</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Artless (1936)Several of the Solomon Islands have a worship of Sea Spirits beings connected with the bonito fishing. Within this worldview, the boundary between human, animal and spirit was often far more fluid than modern Western ideas would suggest. A shark might be viewed not merely as an animal but as a visible expression of a supernatural presence. A dangerous stretch of water might be associated with a spirit's influence rather than an impersonal natural hazard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://anglicanhistory.org/oceania/artless1936/02.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: anglicanhistory.org">[Anglican History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">anglicanhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Anglican History The Church in Melanesia, edited by Stuart W</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Artless (1936)Several of the Solomon Islands have a worship of Sea Spirits beings connected with the bonito fishing. This helps explain why many sea-spirit traditions later appeared mysterious to outside observers. Stories that originally concerned spiritual relationships with the ocean could easily be reinterpreted as tales of strange marine creatures.

## Sharks, Ancestors and Spiritual Authority

The adaro was not the only supernatural sea being known in the eastern Solomon Islands. Regional traditions contain numerous stories linking sharks with ancestry, protection and supernatural power.

One notable example is the figure Karemanua, a part-human, part-shark being known across parts of the eastern Solomon Islands. Accounts collected by anthropologists and museum researchers show that this figure occupied an important place in local mythology and ceremonial life. Carved representations of shark-related spiritual beings survived into the twentieth century in some traditional structures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/vengeful-spirits-and-guardian-dieties/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: penn.museum">[penn.museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">penn.museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on penn.museum.</span></span></span>

These traditions demonstrate that shark symbolism was not simply about fear. Sharks could represent lineage, protection, status and connections between living communities and the spirit world. In some cases, families traced important spiritual relationships through particular marine beings, creating a cultural landscape in which sea creatures possessed meanings extending far beyond biology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/vengeful-spirits-and-guardian-dieties/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: penn.museum">[penn.museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">penn.museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on penn.museum.</span></span></span>

For that reason, stories about adaro and related beings are better understood alongside ancestral traditions than alongside reports of unknown animals.

## Folklore Versus Cryptid Interpretations

The modern internet has blurred the distinction between folklore and cryptozoology. Online creature databases frequently classify the adaro as a cryptid or sea monster, presenting traditional descriptions as though they were eyewitness accounts of a biological species.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cryptidz.fandom.com/wiki/Adaro" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cryptidz.fandom.com">[Cryptid Wiki]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cryptidz.fandom.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cryptid Wiki Adaro &#124; Cryptid Wiki</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cryptid Wiki Adaro &#124; Cryptid Wiki</span></span></span>

Historically, however, the evidence points in a different direction. The earliest recorded discussions come from ethnographic and linguistic work documenting local beliefs, rituals and oral traditions. These sources describe the adaro as a spiritual being embedded within a wider religious framework rather than a hidden marine animal awaiting scientific discovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaro_%28mythology%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Adaro (mythology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">January 22, 2026 — Adaro is a term for two distinct classes of beings found in the mythology of Makira island, in the Solomon Islands. ## Why Sea Spirits Were Sometimes Mistaken for Sea Monsters

When European missionaries, travellers and later cryptid enthusiasts encountered Solomon Islands traditions, they often interpreted them through their own expectations. A story about a shark-spirit, ancestor-being or ghostly sea dweller could be retold as a tale of a mysterious creature inhabiting the ocean.

The adaro's vivid appearance encouraged this process. A being with fins, gills and a shark-like horn sounds superficially similar to the merfolk and sea monsters found in many other cultures. ## A Different Kind of Mystery

For readers interested in the strange traditions of the Solomon Islands, the adaro remains fascinating precisely because it sits at the boundary between folklore and cryptid culture. Unlike reports of giant humanoids or alleged unknown animals, the adaro was never primarily a claim about undiscovered wildlife. It was part of a complex way of understanding the ocean, ancestry and unseen forces.

That distinction offers an important lesson for the wider study of mystery creatures. Some legends begin with alleged encounters with unusual animals. Others begin as spiritual narratives that later acquire a monster-like reputation. The adaro belongs firmly in the second category: a sea spirit whose story reveals far more about island beliefs and relationships with the ocean than about hidden creatures beneath the waves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaro_%28mythology%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Adaro (mythology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">January 22, 2026 — Adaro is a term for two distinct classes of beings found in the mythology of Makira island, in the Solomon Islands.
