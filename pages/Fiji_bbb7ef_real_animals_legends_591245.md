## Introduction

Fiji’s monster lore often looks supernatural on the surface, but many of its most enduring sea-creature stories have roots in remarkably real animals. The islands sit within one of the richest marine environments in the Pacific, surrounded by coral reefs, mangrove forests, estuaries and river mouths that support sharks, rays, eels and other large predators. In particular, the bull shark—a species capable of moving between salt water and fresh water—helps explain why stories of powerful shark spirits, river guardians and dangerous creatures became so deeply woven into Fijian tradition. Modern marine biology has not reduced these stories to simple misunderstandings. Instead, it shows how real encounters with impressive animals provided the raw material from which legends grew.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6635934/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Essential waters: Young bull sharks in Fiji&#x27;s largest riverine...by KBJ Glaus · 2019 · Cited by 26 — Young bull sharks occur in all t...</span></span></span>

## Why Fiji's Monsters Come From the Sea

Unlike countries whose mystery-animal traditions focus on forests or mountains, Fiji's stories emerged from a world where people depended on reefs, lagoons and coastal waters for travel, food and survival. For generations, dangerous channels, sudden storms and encounters with large marine animals demanded respect. A shark appearing where none was expected, a giant ray gliding beneath a canoe, or an eel emerging from a riverbank could easily become attached to spiritual meanings.

This helps explain why figures such as [Dakuwaqa]({{ 'dakuwaqa/' | relative_url }}), the famous shark guardian of Fijian tradition, were not imagined as random monsters. They reflected the realities of living beside powerful marine predators. Dakuwaqa's role as both protector and potential threat mirrors the complicated relationship coastal communities had with sharks themselves—animals that inspired fear, respect and admiration at the same time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://australian.museum/publications/sharks/fiji/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: australian.museum">[australian.museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">australian.museum</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Australian Museum Fiji</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Australian MuseumFiji - The Australian MuseumSCUBA divers dive with Bull Sharks in Fiji. ## Bull Sharks, Rivers and Estuaries

The strongest real-world candidate behind many stories of river sharks and shark spirits is the bull shark (*Carcharhinus leucas*). Unlike most sharks, bull sharks tolerate fresh water and routinely move far upriver. Around the world they have been recorded in rivers, lakes and estuaries, and Fiji is one of the places where their use of river systems has been studied in detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6635934/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Essential waters: Young bull sharks in Fiji&#x27;s largest riverine...by KBJ Glaus · 2019 · Cited by 26 — Young bull sharks occur in all t...</span></span></span>

Research in Fiji's major rivers has shown that young bull sharks regularly inhabit freshwater and estuarine environments. Studies found juveniles in the Rewa, Navua and Sigatoka river systems, with the Rewa River appearing particularly important as nursery habitat for newborn sharks. Scientists captured, tagged and released large numbers of neonate bull sharks in the Lower Rewa River and Delta, confirming that these waterways function as genuine shark nurseries rather than occasional visitation sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6635934/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Essential waters: Young bull sharks in Fiji&#x27;s largest riverine...by KBJ Glaus · 2019 · Cited by 26 — Young bull sharks occur in all t...</span></span></span>

For traditional communities, such animals would have been extraordinary. A large shark appearing in muddy river water far from the open sea could easily seem supernatural. Even today, many people unfamiliar with bull shark biology find the idea of sharks in rivers surprising. Before modern zoology explained the phenomenon, river-dwelling sharks would have fitted naturally into stories of guardians, shape-shifters or dangerous water spirits.

The ecological picture is even broader than a single river. Surveys have identified potential bull shark nursery areas in several Fijian river systems, while the Dreketi River and other estuaries support diverse shark and ray populations. Fiji's rivers are therefore not merely routes connecting land and sea; they are habitats where marine predators genuinely live during important stages of their lives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wwfasia.awsassets.panda.org/downloads/dreketi_river___estuary_shark_and_ray_survey_report_centrespread.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wwfasia.awsassets.panda.org">[panda.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wwfasia.awsassets.panda.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on panda.org.</span></span></span>

## When a Shark Looks Like a Spirit

Many traditional stories are easier to understand once the behaviour of bull sharks is considered. Bull sharks often enter murky water where visibility is poor. They can appear suddenly, vanish quickly and travel between environments that people once regarded as separate worlds.

A fisher encountering such an animal repeatedly in a particular river bend might conclude that a guardian spirit lived there. A shark seen at the mouth of a river after a storm could become associated with omens or warnings. Over generations, repeated observations could merge with oral traditions until the biological animal and the supernatural being became difficult to separate.

Modern studies around Fiji also reveal that bull sharks are not simple wandering predators. Researchers have documented site fidelity, meaning sharks often return to particular areas repeatedly. Recent genetic work has even shown evidence that female bull sharks return to the same reproductive regions across generations. ## Rays, Eels and Other Animals Behind Strange Sightings

Sharks are not the only real creatures that contributed to Fiji's monster traditions.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://hai.swiss/en/projects/completed/bull-sharks-fiji" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hai.swiss">[hai.swiss]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hai.swiss</span><span class="citation-popover-title">bull sharks fiji</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fiji is ideal to study bull sharks. Large rays can create dramatic surface disturbances and fleeting silhouettes. Seen briefly from a canoe or shoreline, a ray's broad body and wing-like fins may resemble an unfamiliar creature. In poor light, observers can struggle to judge size and shape accurately.

Large eels provide another likely source of folklore. Tropical river systems throughout the Pacific contain sizeable eels capable of reaching impressive lengths. Their snake-like appearance makes them natural candidates for stories involving river monsters or mysterious water beings. A glimpse of an eel moving through muddy water can look far larger than it really is.

Even ordinary reef fish can become extraordinary under certain conditions. Refraction, waves, shadows and changing water clarity often distort size and distance. A large fish viewed from above may seem enormous, while a group of fish moving together can appear to be a single creature. Such effects are common ingredients in mystery-animal reports worldwide and likely contributed to some Fijian sea-monster tales as well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.vogue.com/article/fiji-sustainable-tourism" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vogue.com">[Vogue]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vogue.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How to Visit Fiji and Leave Its Islands Better Than You Found Them</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Known for its hospitality, Fiji has embraced regenerative travel and conservation, making it one of the most sustainable destinations in...</span></span></span>

## Misreadings, Sightings and Marine Biology

The most interesting lesson from Fiji's mystery-creature traditions is not that people were simply mistaken. Rather, the legends often contain accurate observations embedded within cultural interpretation.

A report of a shark spirit inhabiting a river may preserve genuine knowledge that sharks regularly use that waterway. Stories warning people away from particular channels may reflect places where large predators are frequently encountered. Narratives describing powerful sea guardians may echo the ecological importance of apex predators that shape marine ecosystems.

Scientific research increasingly supports the idea that Fiji's rivers and estuaries are crucial habitats for young bull sharks and other marine life. Mangroves, river deltas and sheltered estuarine environments provide safe nursery grounds where juvenile sharks can grow before moving into coastal waters. ## From Monsters to Conservation Symbols

An unexpected twist in the story is that many animals once feared as monsters have become symbols of conservation. Bull sharks now attract researchers and ecotourists to Fiji, while shark-focused conservation projects frequently draw upon cultural traditions linked to Dakuwaqa and other shark figures.
